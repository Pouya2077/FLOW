About flow

flow (First-responder Logistics Over Water) shows first responders and residents which streets have water on them, and how much of each, from free satellite radar.

{% lucide "lightbulb" size=20 aria_hidden="true" %} Inspiration

In November 2021, atmospheric rivers flooded Abbotsford and the Sumas Prairie in British Columbia's Fraser Valley. We remember the damage to farms, homes and roads there, and how far it spread: with highways and rail lines cut, food deliveries across the province lagged for weeks.

If it was hard for the rest of us, it must have been far harder for the first responders working inside the flooded area, deciding which roads they could still use.

Floods like this happen around the world, and radar satellites pass over them, seeing through clouds day and night. That data is free and public, and could tell residents and first responders about the dangers in front of them. So we set out to build something for them with it.

{% lucide "target" size=20 aria_hidden="true" %} Our goal

An app that lets first responders and residents see where water has been detected on the roads they use, and around the critical buildings in their area: hospitals, fire and police stations, schools, power substations and dykes.

And to find a path between those places that keeps the risk as low as possible: avoiding roads where water was seen, and flagging any route that uses roads the satellite didn't observe.

{% lucide "layers" size=20 aria_hidden="true" %} How we built it

The core idea is an overlay: a map of where the satellite saw water, laid over the road map. Each road is cut into short pieces and each piece is checked against the water beneath it, so a 10 km street flooded for 8 km shows exactly those 8 km in blue.

  • Sentinel-1 radar (Copernicus, free): it sees through cloud and at night. We compare a pass during the flood with an earlier one, because smooth dry pavement can look like water on its own.
  • OpenStreetMap via OSMnx: free, worldwide roads and buildings with the street names needed for per-street totals.
  • rasterio, GeoPandas and Shapely: open Python libraries that read the radar data and measure roads in metres.
  • NetworkX: routing on the same road network, so routes use exactly our flood scoring.
  • Django and Django Ninja: one small server for the page and its data.
  • MapLibre GL and OpenFreeMap: an open map with no API keys or limits, and muted basemaps so the blue water traces stand out.

Every road has one of three states: water detected, observed clear, or not observed. A road the satellite didn't see is flagged for cautionary use.

{% lucide "info" size=20 aria_hidden="true" %} Radar shows where water is, not how deep it is, and the map is only as fresh as the latest satellite pass. During local development, the latest satellite data we have access to is three days old. In a real environment we can expect quicker satellite responses.
